### 1. Introduciton
Autoimmune and chronic inflammatory diseases arise when the immune system mistakenly targets the body’s own tissues, leading to sustained inflammation and tissue damage. Common examples include rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and multiple sclerosis. These conditions are characterized by dysregulated immune signaling, excessive cytokine release, and chronic activation of T and B lymphocytes. Environmental triggers, such as infections, pollutants, and toxins, can exacerbate immune dysregulation and influence disease onset or progression. 

Indoor mold thrives in humid, poorly ventilated environments, especially in regions with high precipitation or frequent flooding. In the United States, mold contamination is most prevalent in areas where warm, moist climates and older housing stock work together to allow water intrusion and microbial growth. Mold exposure is problematic because it can lead to respiratory irritation, allergic sensitization, and systemic inflammation, particularly among individuals with compromised immune systems or chronic disease. Beyond allergic reactions, chronic exposure to mycotoxins may act as an environmental modifier of autoimmune disease risk, influencing disease severity and progression through sustained immune activation and oxidative stress.

Mold exposure, particularly in water‑damaged indoor environments, has been increasingly recognized as a potential contributor to immune dysfunction. Molds release mycotoxins and volatile organic compounds that can provoke inflammatory and allergic responses. Recent studies suggest that chronic exposure to these agents may amplify cytokine and chemokine signaling, disrupt immune tolerance, and even trigger autoantibody formation. For example, Rosenblum Lichtenstein et al. (2015) demonstrated that peripheral blood mononuclear cells from mold‑exposed individuals released significantly higher levels of pro‑inflammatory cytokines when challenged with mold extracts compared to controls. Similarly, Gray et al. (2003) found elevated autoantibodies and altered lymphocyte phenotypes among adults exposed to mixed molds in water‑damaged buildings, indicating heightened autoimmune activity. Tuuminen and Rinne (2017) further reported increased autoimmune disease prevalence in Finnish cohorts exposed to indoor molds, underscoring the potential systemic consequences of chronic exposure. 

We hypothesize that mold exposure contributes to the development of inflammatory diseases, mainly rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and multiple sclerosis. This proposal focuses on adults residing in one of four U.S. regions (Southwest, South, Midwest, or Northeast) who provided information on both self‑report household mold exposure in the All of Us Environmental and Housing Survey and autoimmune or chronic inflammatory disease diagnoses in their All of Us survey responses. This population enables examination of regional environmental variability and its potential role in modulating the relationship between mold exposure and inflammatory disease burden.   

## 2. Specific Aim
To investigate the association between mold exposure and inflammatory diseases, we propose three specific aims: 

Aim 1: Explore the association between mold exposure and incident inflammatory diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis,  psoriasis, and multiple sclerosis, among adult participants in the All of Us Research Program. Findings from this aim will provide foundational evidence for the role of mold as a potential environmental factor that increases the risk of inflammatory disease.

Aim 2: Identify demographic and social determinants of health that modify the association between mold exposure and incident inflammatory diseases. Findings will characterize populations that may be particularly vulnerable to the potential health effects of mold exposure.

Aim 3: Assess regional variation in the association between mold exposure and inflammatory diseases across four U.S. regions: the Pacific Northwest(), the Southwest (California, Arizona, and Texas), South (Florida, Georgia, and Alabama), Midwest (Michigan, Illinois, and Wisconsin), and Northeast (New York, Pennsylvania, and Massachusetts). States were selected to avoid borderline regions, under the assumption that they are representative of each respective region.  Results will provide insight into geographic disparities and environmental conditions that contribute to disease risk.

Completion of these aims will advance understanding of mold exposure as a potential environmental risk factor for inflammatory disease, identify populations at greatest risk, and reveal geographic trends that can inform targeted interventions. 
 
### 3.Methods
We conduct a retrospective cohort study using data from the All of Us Research Program. The study population consists of participants aged ≥18 years residing in the Southwest, South, Midwest, or the Northeast of the US. Participants with missing information on either the exposure or outcome are excluded from the analytic population. The primary outcome is inflammatory disease, specifically rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and multiple sclerosis, which is defined using phecodes. The primary exposure is household mold exposure, which is assessed using self-reported information from the Social Factors Survey. The mold exposure level is corroborated using proxy measures, including housing conditions, water leaks, and potentially indoor air quality, as well as environmental data from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. Potential confounding variables and effect modifiers will be identified based on previous literature. Potential confounders include age, sex, drinking, smoking, and education. Potential effect modifiers include income, health insurance coverage, housing type, and neighborhood condition.

Logistic regression is used to investigate the association. First, we fit an unadjusted logistic regression model to estimate the crude association. We then fit a multivariable logistic regression model adjusting for potential confounders. The associations are quantified using odds ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Subsequently, we investigate whether selected factors modified the association. We extend the multivariable logistic regression model by including interaction terms between mold exposure and each potential effect modifier. Statistical evidence for effect modification is evaluated based on the interaction terms. Finally, the spatial distribution of mold exposure and inflammatory disease is assessed by mapping the prevalence of mold exposure and inflammatory disease across the study regions. We then evaluate regional variation in the association by estimating region-specific regression coefficients
